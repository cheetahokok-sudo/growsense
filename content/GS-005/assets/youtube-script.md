You're at a birthday party, and it hits you: your kid is a head shorter than

**[0:30 · PROMISE]**
In the next few minutes I'll give you the exact framework a pediatric
endocrinologist uses — so you can tell an ordinary short child from the rare
case that genuinely needs a doctor. Everything's sourced on screen. No fear, no

**[0:55 · THE PERCENTILE PARADOX]**
First, "short stature" has a real definition: below the 3rd percentile, or more
than 2 standard deviations below average. `[TEXT: <3rd percentile / −2 SD · ref 1, 3]`
But here's the trap: being on the 3rd percentile is **not a disease.** By
definition, 3 out of every 100 perfectly healthy kids sit there. Someone always
has to. `[VISUAL: growth chart, 100 kids, 3 highlighted at the bottom]`

**[1:40 · STOP COMPARING SIDEWAYS]**
The biggest mistake parents make is comparing their child to classmates. Doctors
don't. They compare your child to **their own history and their own genetics.**
Shortest in the class is a social fact. Falling off their own curve is a medical
one. Only the second matters.

**[2:20 · VELOCITY BEATS HEIGHT]**
If you remember one thing, make it this: **how fast** a child grows matters more
than how tall they are today. `[VISUAL: Scenario A vs B — short +5cm/yr healthy, tall +2cm/yr concerning]`
A short child growing five centimeters a year is fine. A taller child who's
slowed to two? That's the one I want to see. `[TEXT: growth velocity · ref 6]`
And measure over 6 to 12 months — one number at a school health day tells you
almost nothing.

**[3:20 · YOUR FAMILY'S ZONE]**
Genetics drives 60 to 80 percent of height. `[TEXT: 60–80% heritable · ref 10]`
There's a simple mid-parental formula that gives a **range** — most kids finish
within about 8-and-a-half centimeters of the centre. `[VISUAL: mid-parental formula + ±8.5 cm band]`
The right question isn't "are they near average?" It's "are they tracking toward
**our family's** zone?"

**[4:10 · THE LATE BLOOMER]**
A huge number of short-but-healthy kids fall into two normal patterns: familial
short stature — short parents — or **constitutional delay**, the late bloomer.
`[VISUAL: two-column table — familial vs late bloomer]`
The late-bloomer signature: normal growth rate, a family history of late
development, and a **delayed bone age.** `[TEXT: bone age · ref 9]` Their growth
plates stay open longer, so they catch up in their late teens.

**[5:10 · THE REAL RED FLAGS]**
So when *should* you see a doctor? `[VISUAL: red-flag checklist]`
Crossing percentiles downward — 50th, to 25th, to 10th. `[TEXT: ref 15]`
Velocity under about 4 cm a year. `[TEXT: ref 2]`
No puberty by 13 in girls or 14 in boys. `[TEXT: ref 7]`
Or slow growth **plus** feeling unwell. The rule: a short child who's well and
growing steadily can be watched; one who's slowing, crossing lines, or unwell
should be seen.

**[6:10 · WHAT THE VISIT IS LIKE + GH TRUTH]**
And the visit isn't scary — it's a chat, a measurement, sometimes a quick
hand X-ray and a blood test. `[TEXT: history · plotting · bone age · bloods · ref 4, 5]`
For most kids it ends with the commonest diagnosis of all: a healthy child who's
short. One honest note on growth-hormone shots — they're a real medicine for
real diagnoses, but for otherwise-healthy short kids the average gain is small
and the guidelines are deliberately cautious. `[TEXT: GH — modest gain in ISS · ref 2, 3]`
It's a treatment for a condition, not a height upgrade.

**[7:10 · THE FEAR UNDER THE FEAR + CTA]**
Last thing, and it matters: research shows shorter kids grow up just as happy
and well-adjusted as taller ones. `[TEXT: psychosocial adjustment indistinguishable · ref 17]`
The thing kids absorb most isn't a classmate's comment — it's a parent's
anxiety. So track velocity, ask the right questions, and treat your child as the
whole, capable person they are. That's exactly why we built GrowSense. Link
